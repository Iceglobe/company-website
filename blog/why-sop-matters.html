Why S&OP Matters for Manufacturers, and How to Start

S&OP is one of the few processes that reaches almost every department in a company. Get it right and you see it in the numbers, and in how people work together.

TL;DR

S&OP turns a forecast into a plan operations can deliver and gets sales, operations, supply chain and finance working from the same picture. Start with a monthly cycle, let margin decide whether sales or operations has the final say, and keep highly variable, high-volume items out of the forecast by making them to order.

Watch the 9-minute video this article is based on, or see the full S&OP training series.

What S&OP actually gives you

Sales and operations planning has the potential to reach just about every department in a company. Wherever you sit, odds are you will be asked for information or for help with a problem at some point in the cycle. That is exactly what makes it valuable: finance, sales, operations, supply chain and product marketing end up looking at the same picture.

The hard metrics are the part everyone expects:

  • Leaner inventory, because a sharper forecast needs less buffer.
  • Earlier problem solving and risk management, because issues show up months ahead instead of on the production floor.
  • Less scrap and lower cost across operations.
  • Happier customers, because you can deliver what was promised.
  • A better-equipped sales team that knows what is possible and what isn't.

The less obvious gain is cultural. A good S&OP process unites people behind a common view of the challenges, how they are being addressed, what the next steps are and who needs to be involved. For most production companies that combination is a huge unlock.

Where demand planning fits in the full planning cycle

This article covers the first step of a good planning process: demand planning and the governance around it, meaning the S&OP review and the sign-off you run with your biggest customers or sales affiliates. Everything else feeds from it:

  • Supply planning, inventory management and capacity planning turn the agreed demand into orders, stock targets and production loads.
  • S&OE (sales and operations execution) checks how you are actually executing: delivery performance, and where risks are building for the coming weeks.
  • Executive S&OP condenses everything before it into a sharp view of the whole business, and brings in senior management where their decisions are needed.

You may hear the most mature form of this called integrated business planning (IBP). The line between a very mature S&OP and IBP is fluid. The terminology matters less than doing the right activities every month to keep the business united behind a common purpose.

The two jobs S&OP is there to do

  1. Create a forecast operations can actually deliver to, so you can serve customers as promised.
  2. Unite people across departments, so everyone knows what is being worked on, what their role in the solution is, and when they need to pay attention.

Everything else is useful extra on top of those two.

How often should you run S&OP?

Not every S&OP process looks the same. A global pharma company and a small workshop on the corner will run very different cycles, and need is what should drive the frequency. As a rule of thumb, once a month is a good starting point for most manufacturers. Some businesses need a weekly or twice-monthly rhythm, others can work quarterly. Start monthly and adjust once you see where the plan breaks.

Who gets the loudest voice: sales or operations?

Every S&OP process has a dominant voice, and margin largely decides who it should be.

  • High-margin business: every sale counts, and you can absorb higher operating costs such as overtime or air freight instead of sea freight to get product to the customer faster. Here sales should have the loudest voice, because sales is what needs to be accommodated.
  • Low-margin, high-volume business: you make money on efficiency. Overtime, small batches or expensive distribution can be the difference between earning and losing money on an order. Here operations should have the final word on what is doable.

Not every item should be forecast

Focus the S&OP process on what can reasonably be forecast. Picture a chart with volume on one axis and demand variability on the other:

  • High volume, high variability: these orders come in rarely and in large quantities, and they throw off operations every time. Where the customer accepts your production lead time, make them to order.
  • The band just below: items on the border between make-to-order and make-to-stock. Decide where they go with clear rules, not case by case. With 10,000 items, case by case is not a viable way to work.
  • Everything else: volume and variability give you a pattern you can forecast and plan with. This is where S&OP spends its time.
